## Where it comes from

Taylorism, or *scientific management*, is the body of ideas developed by Frederick Winslow Taylor (1856–1915) and published most completely in *The Principles of Scientific Management* (1911). Taylor was not an academic. He started as a machinist's apprentice, became a gang boss at Midvale Steel, and later a consultant at Bethlehem Steel. Everything he wrote came out of standing on a shop floor with a stopwatch.

The world he was writing for matters. American industry around 1900 was growing faster than it could be managed. Factories were filling with workers who had no craft tradition behind them, often immigrants with no shared language. Work was organised by "rule of thumb": the craftsman knew how to do the job, the foreman knew roughly how long it should take, and nobody could say exactly why one man produced twice as much as the next. Output per worker varied wildly and management had no instrument to explain the variation.

Taylor's starting diagnosis was what he called **systematic soldiering**. Workers deliberately held back their output. He did not think this made them bad people — he thought it was rational. Under the piece-rate systems of the day, if a worker produced more, management responded by cutting the rate per piece. The worker ended up doing more work for the same money, and soon put his colleagues out of a job. So the group agreed, informally and durably, on a pace well below what was possible. Management suspected this and could never prove it, because management did not actually know how long the work took.

## The core claim

Taylor's answer was that the time a task takes is not a matter of opinion. It is a measurable fact, and it can be established by investigation. Once you know it, the argument between worker and manager about "a fair day's work" disappears, because both sides are now looking at the same number.

He generalised this into the claim that for every piece of work there exists **one best way** to do it — one best sequence of motions, one best tool, one best load, one best rest interval — and that this best way can be discovered by breaking the work into elements, timing each element, discarding the wasteful ones, and reassembling what remains.

The phrase itself is more strongly associated with Frank and Lillian Gilbreth, who made "the one best way" the slogan of motion study. Taylor's own usage tended toward "the best method" for a given task. The concept belongs to both.

## The four principles

Taylor set out four duties of management.

1. **Develop a science for each element of a man's work**, replacing the old rule-of-thumb method. Study the work itself, write down the laws governing it.
2. **Scientifically select, then train, teach and develop the workman.** Previously the worker chose his own work and trained himself as best he could. Taylor wanted the right person matched to the task and then deliberately taught.
3. **Heartily cooperate with the men** so as to ensure the work is done in accordance with the principles of the science that has been developed. Having a method on paper is worthless if the shop floor ignores it.
4. **Divide the work and the responsibility almost equally between management and workmen.** Management takes over all the work for which it is better fitted — planning, method, scheduling — and leaves execution to the worker.

The fourth principle is the one later commentators and critics have concentrated on. It is the formal separation of **thinking about the work** from **doing the work**. Taylor was explicit: "All possible brain work should be removed from the shop and centred in the planning or laying-out department."

## The machinery

The principles came with concrete apparatus, and the apparatus is what organisations actually copied.

- **Time and motion study.** The stopwatch, the element sheet, the elimination of unnecessary movement. Frank and Lillian Gilbreth developed the motion side further, filming work and reducing it to elementary motions they named *therbligs*.
- **Standardisation of tools and conditions.** At Bethlehem, Taylor found that shovels were whatever the workers brought from home. He established that the optimal shovel load was about 21 pounds and had shovels of different sizes made for different materials, so that every load was the same weight.
- **The instruction card.** Each worker receives a written card specifying the task, the method and the time allowed. The card, not the foreman's memory, is the authority.
- **The differential piece rate.** A high rate per piece if the standard is met, a markedly lower rate if it is not. The incentive is deliberately steep so that the worker's interest is attached to the standard.
- **Functional foremanship.** Taylor broke the single foreman into eight specialised roles — gang boss, speed boss, inspector, repair boss, order-of-work clerk, instruction-card clerk, time-and-cost clerk, shop disciplinarian. Each worker therefore reported to several specialists, each in their own domain. This is the one piece of Taylor's system that was rarely adopted intact.
- **The planning department.** A separate organ that holds the method, the schedule and the routing, and feeds instructions down to the floor.

## The famous demonstrations

Taylor's own case studies did most of the persuading, and most of the arguing.

The **pig iron** story at Bethlehem: handlers were loading about 12.5 tons of pig iron per man per day. By selecting a worker Taylor called Schmidt, prescribing exactly when to walk, when to lift and when to sit down, output rose to about 47.5 tons per day, with the worker's pay rising from $1.15 to $1.85. Taylor presented it as proof that the method, not effort, was the binding constraint.

The **shovelling** study: by standardising the load, Bethlehem reportedly reduced the yard gang from around 500 men to 140 while raising output, at substantially higher wages for those who remained.

The **metal-cutting** experiments: twenty-six years of work on cutting speeds and feeds, which incidentally produced high-speed tool steel. This was Taylor's own favourite evidence that work could be reduced to law.

The **bricklaying** work of Gilbreth: motion analysis reduced the motions per brick from eighteen to about four and a half for exterior brick, and as few as two for interior brick, with reported output rising from 120 to 350 bricks per man per hour. Much of the saving came from eliminating stooping — roughly 125 stoops an hour for brick and another 125 for mortar — by raising the materials to working height.

## What the historical record says about those demonstrations

The case studies above are Taylor's own accounts, and they have not survived scrutiny intact.

Charles Wrege and Amedeo Perroni examined the pig iron episode against primary sources in "Taylor's Pig-Tale: A Historical Analysis of Frederick W. Taylor's Pig-Iron Experiments" (*Academy of Management Journal*, 1974). They concluded that the story, accepted for decades without question, was substantially constructed after the fact: that Taylor drew general laws from data that did not support them, and assembled the narrative for persuasive effect. Their summary was that "whether imaginative or impudent, the fact is that Taylor seems to have believed that the end justified the means." Wrege returned to the material with Richard Hodgetts in 2000, concluding that what the typical management reader "knows" about Bethlehem is largely fiction. The worker Taylor called Schmidt was Henry Noll.

The shovelling figures carry the same caveat. The 21-pound optimum and the reduction in the size of the yard gang come from Taylor's own reporting, and the surrounding detail has been questioned on the same grounds.

This does not establish that scientific management did not work, and plenty of firms recorded genuine productivity gains under it. What it establishes is narrower and more specific: the founding empirical demonstrations of measurement-based management were themselves reported in a way that would not pass the standard they advocated. Any account of Taylor that repeats the tonnages as settled fact is repeating Taylor rather than the record.

## What Taylorism assumes

The following is an analytical reading rather than a list Taylor set out himself. Underneath the technique sits a set of assumptions. They are usually unstated, which is exactly why they travel so well into contexts Taylor never imagined.

- Work can be decomposed into elements that are **independent** of each other and of who performs them.
- The best method is **stable** enough to be worth writing down, and the environment changes slowly enough for the written method to stay true.
- Knowledge of how the work should be done properly belongs in a **separate** function from the people doing it.
- Workers are **interchangeable** once trained to the standard; the unit of planning is a quantity of labour, not a particular person.
- The primary motivator is **economic**; pay the standard and the behaviour follows.
- What is not measured is not managed, and what cannot be measured is roughly assumed not to matter.
- Variation is **waste**. Deviation from the standard is a defect to be eliminated, not information to be examined.

## How it spread

Scientific management spread far beyond machine shops. Henry Ford's moving assembly line (1913) took the division of labour further than Taylor ever did. Henry Gantt, one of Taylor's own associates, produced the chart that still bears his name. Lenin read Taylor and pushed his methods into Soviet industry. Hospitals, schools, government departments and offices all adopted the vocabulary: standard times, output quotas, work study, the separation of planning from doing.

Clerical work was Taylorised early. The typing pool, the keystroke count and the standard handling time per form are scientific management applied to paper. The call centre, with its average handling time, its scripts and its adherence targets, follows the same pattern.

Parallels are frequently drawn between these mechanisms and several now standard in software delivery: the ticket and the instruction card, estimation and time study, velocity and output per period, utilisation targets as the measure of a working day, and the hand-off from analysis to build to test as a form of functional specialisation. These are structural resemblances rather than a documented line of descent — the practices in software arrived by their own routes, and whether the resemblance amounts to inheritance is a matter of interpretation.

## The objections that arrived with it

Resistance was immediate and it was not only ideological.

Time study began at the **Watertown Arsenal** in February 1911 and the molders struck that August. The US House of Representatives authorised a Special Committee to Investigate the Taylor and Other Systems of Shop Management, before which Taylor testified on 25 January 1912. The prohibition on stopwatch timing and premium payments in government establishments followed in the 1915 Army Appropriations Act, and was renewed in appropriations legislation for some decades afterwards. Organised labour read the system as a device to intensify work and break craft control.

The deeper critique was articulated much later by Harry Braverman in *Labor and Monopoly Capital* (1974), as **deskilling**: the separation of conception from execution is not a side effect of Taylorism but its purpose. When method is held centrally, the skill leaves the worker, the job becomes cheaper to fill, and control shifts permanently to management.

A separate line of objection came from the **Hawthorne** studies at Western Electric (1924–1932). Researchers set out to measure the effect of physical conditions such as lighting on output and reported that the social situation — being observed, being part of a group, the informal norms of that group — dominated the physical variables they were manipulating. The studies put the social dimension of work on the agenda in a way Taylor's model had no place for, and their influence on management thought is not in doubt.

Their evidential basis is. Steven Levitt and John List located the original illumination-experiment records and re-analysed them in "Was There Really a Hawthorne Effect at the Hawthorne Plant?" (*American Economic Journal: Applied Economics*, 2011). They found that the dramatic data patterns described in the standard accounts are not present: output rose with brighter light, with dimmer light, with no change in light, and after the experiment had ended, and tracked day of the week and the pay-period cycle more closely than it tracked the lighting at all. They do find weaker effects consistent with some influence of observation. The later relay-assembly and interviewing phases rest on separate evidence and are not addressed by that re-analysis.

The pattern is worth noting on both sides of the argument: the founding demonstration of scientific management and the study most often cited against it have both been found, on examination of the original records, to have been reported more confidently than the data warranted.

Taylor himself insisted he was misrepresented. He argued that his system raised wages, that it ended the arbitrary authority of the foreman, and that the "mental revolution" he asked for was mutual — management had to give up its guesswork too. He was consistent that the method had to be developed *with* the workers, not imposed on them. What organisations adopted, in his lifetime and ever since, was usually the stopwatch and the standard without the mental revolution.
