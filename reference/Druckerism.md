## Where it comes from

Druckerism is the body of thinking developed by Peter F. Drucker (1909–2005) over roughly sixty years and forty books. He was born in Vienna, worked as a journalist and economist in Germany and England, and moved to the United States in 1937. He was not an engineer and never ran a company. He described himself as a writer and a teacher, and late in life as a "social ecologist" — someone who studies the human-made environment the way an ecologist studies the natural one.

The decisive piece of fieldwork came in 1943, when General Motors invited him to study the company from the inside for eighteen months. The result, *Concept of the Corporation* (1946), treated the large corporation as a social institution rather than an economic mechanism — as the place where most people in an industrial society would spend their working lives, and therefore as something that had to be made to work in human terms, not only in financial ones. GM's management disliked the book. It made Drucker's reputation.

*The Practice of Management* (1954) is where the discipline gets assembled. *Managing for Results* (1964), *The Effective Executive* (1966), *The Age of Discontinuity* (1969), *Innovation and Entrepreneurship* (1985), *Post-Capitalist Society* (1993) and *Management Challenges for the 21st Century* (1999) extend it.

Where Taylor starts with the task and reasons toward the organisation, Drucker starts with the organisation as a human institution and reasons toward the task. That difference in starting point produces nearly everything else.

## Management as a discipline

Drucker's first claim is that management is a distinct function with its own body of knowledge, not a by-product of ownership, seniority or technical skill. Before him, "manager" largely meant "the person whose business it is". He argued it is a practice — like medicine or law — that can be studied, taught and done well or badly.

He also insisted that management is a **liberal art**: "liberal" because it deals with knowledge, self-knowledge, wisdom and leadership; "art" because it is practice and application. A manager draws on psychology, history, economics and ethics, and is judged by results in the world.

The manager's job, in his formulation, is to **make people's strengths effective and their weaknesses irrelevant**. Organisations exist precisely because no individual has all the capability required; the point of organising is to combine strengths, not to repair deficiencies.

## The purpose of a business

Drucker rejected profit maximisation as the purpose of a business. Profit, in his account, is a *condition* of survival and a *test* of performance — the cost of staying in business and the cost of the future — but it explains nothing about what the business is for and gives no guidance about what to do.

His formulation: **the purpose of a business is to create a customer.** From which follow his two functions:

> "Because its purpose is to create a customer, the business enterprise has two — and only these two — basic functions: marketing and innovation. Marketing and innovation produce results; all the rest are costs."

He pushed the same discipline into three questions he returned to for fifty years: *What is our business? Who is the customer? What does the customer consider value?* He treated these as genuinely hard questions, rarely asked, and almost never answered the same way by the customer as by the company.

## The knowledge worker

The concept Drucker is most identified with is the **knowledge worker**. He introduced it in *Landmarks of Tomorrow* (1959) and developed it over the following decade. Sources differ slightly on the coinage: "knowledge work" appears in the 1959 book, which is generally credited as the origin of the idea, while some trace the exact compound "knowledge worker" to *The Effective Executive* (1966).

The definition is structural, not about education or prestige. A knowledge worker is someone who **knows more about their own work than anyone else in the organisation**, including their boss. That single fact breaks the model in which method flows down from a planning function. Nobody upstairs is in a position to write the instruction card.

The consequences Drucker drew from it:

- The knowledge worker **owns the means of production**. Knowledge is between their ears; it is portable; it leaves the building every evening and may not come back. The relationship is therefore closer to a partnership than to employment, and the organisation needs the worker more than the worker needs the organisation.
- Knowledge workers must be **self-managing**. Not as a motivational nicety but because direction from above cannot be sufficiently informed.
- They are **assets, not costs**. Costs are to be minimised; assets are to be grown. Which of the two an organisation genuinely believes shows up in its decisions, not its statements.
- They have to be **attracted and retained**, which means the organisation must be worth working for.

He regarded raising the productivity of knowledge work as "the most important contribution management needs to make in the 21st century" — and compared the opportunity to what Taylor's work did for manual productivity in the twentieth, while being clear the method could not be the same.

## Six requirements for knowledge-worker productivity

In *Management Challenges for the 21st Century* (1999) he set out six factors.

1. **"What is the task?"** For manual work the task is given. For knowledge work it is the central question, and the person doing the work has to answer it. Drucker held that the most important question to ask a knowledge worker is simply this one.
2. **Autonomy.** The worker has to manage themselves. Responsibility for their own productivity rests with them.
3. **Continuous innovation** has to be part of the job, built into the task and the responsibility.
4. **Continuous learning and continuous teaching.** Knowledge workers learn most by teaching — the obligation runs both ways.
5. **Quality is at least as important as quantity**, and often the whole of the output. Counting units is not merely insufficient; it can be actively misleading.
6. **The worker is treated as an asset rather than a cost**, and *wants* to work for this organisation in preference to the alternatives.

He added a further observation: for manual work, the first question is always "how should the work be done?" For knowledge work, that question comes much later, if at all. The first task is to define what the work *is*, and a great deal of knowledge-work improvement comes from eliminating what need not be done at all.

## Management by objectives and self-control

MBO, introduced in *The Practice of Management*, is frequently cited and frequently reduced to its first half. Drucker's full phrase is **"management by objectives and self-control"**, and he regarded the second half as the operative part.

The mechanism: each manager establishes objectives for their own unit, derived from and contributing to the objectives of the larger whole. Objectives are arrived at in conversation with the superior, not handed down. Drucker suggested the **"manager's letter"** — the subordinate writes to the boss setting out what they understand the objectives of their job to be, what they will be held accountable for, what they intend to do, and what obstacles the organisation puts in their way. The frequency with which such a letter surprises the boss is itself the diagnostic.

The **self-control** element is about information. The person doing the work receives the measurements of their own performance, directly and quickly enough to act on them, and uses them to correct themselves. Drucker's point is that this is a different purpose for measurement than appraisal from above: the same number used for control-from-above and self-control produces different behaviour.

He was also clear about the failure mode: objectives that are set centrally and cascaded mechanically are not MBO. They are quotas with a modern vocabulary.

## Effectiveness, not efficiency

Drucker's distinction: **efficiency is doing things right; effectiveness is doing the right things.** Efficiency is about the input/output ratio of an activity; effectiveness is about whether the activity should exist. He held that for knowledge work effectiveness is the scarce resource, and that an organisation can be destroyed by becoming more efficient at the wrong things.

*The Effective Executive* (1966) gives five practices, which he insisted are habits that can be learned rather than traits people are born with:

1. **Know where your time goes.** Record it, manage it, consolidate it into usable blocks. Drucker found that executives' beliefs about how they spent their time bore little relation to the record.
2. **Focus on outward contribution.** Ask "what can I contribute?" rather than "what do I have to do?" — orienting to results outside the organisation rather than effort inside it.
3. **Build on strengths.** Your own, your colleagues', your boss's, and those of the situation. Staffing to avoid weakness produces mediocrity.
4. **First things first.** Concentration on a few priorities, done one at a time, and — equally — the courage to decide what *not* to do.
5. **Make effective decisions.** Which for Drucker meant a small number of decisions at the highest conceptual level, starting from disagreement rather than consensus, with the action to implement and the feedback to verify built in from the start.

To these he attached **feedback analysis**: whenever you make a key decision, write down what you expect to happen; compare against what actually happened nine or twelve months later. He claimed this is the only reliable way to learn what your strengths actually are, as opposed to what you believe them to be.

## Systematic abandonment

Drucker's standing question: *"If we were not already doing this, would we go into it now?"* If the answer is no, the response is not further study but abandonment.

He treated abandonment as a scheduled, organised practice rather than a crisis response — every product, service, process and policy put on trial for its life at regular intervals. His reasoning is about where resources go: the first thing new work requires is capable people, and capable people are always occupied with yesterday's work. Nothing is harder to kill than something that was once a success.

## Decentralisation and the organisation as community

From the GM study onward, Drucker advocated **federal decentralisation**: breaking the organisation into units that are genuinely businesses, each with its own market and its own results, each led by people who carry real authority and real accountability. Where a true business unit is not possible, he described **simulated decentralisation** as an inferior but sometimes necessary substitute.

The arguments he gave were as much developmental as structural. Decentralisation produces managers — it gives people a scope in which they can be measured on results and learn by carrying consequences. Centralised functional organisation produces specialists who have never been responsible for an outcome.

He also wrote persistently about the organisation as a **human community**, especially as the older communities of village, church and extended family weakened. Status, function and belonging, in his analysis, now had to be found largely at work, and an organisation that ignores this is ignoring one of the main things it actually is.

## The information-based organisation

In "The Coming of the New Organization" (*Harvard Business Review*, 1988) Drucker predicted that the large business twenty years out would have roughly half the levels of management and a third the managers of its 1988 counterpart, and would resemble a hospital or a symphony orchestra more than a manufacturing company.

The reasoning: much of middle management exists to relay and aggregate information, and once information moves directly, those layers have no remaining function. What is left is an organisation of specialists who direct themselves through feedback from colleagues, customers and headquarters.

The orchestra analogy carries the point he wanted. Several hundred highly trained specialists play together with one conductor and no intermediate layers. There is no vice-president of woodwinds. Each player holds the score — the shared information about the whole — not just their own part. Co-ordination comes from everyone seeing the same thing, not from instructions passed down.

He was explicit that this kind of organisation makes *greater* demands for clarity, not fewer: on what each person is responsible for, on what information they owe to whom, and on self-discipline. His recurring questions for this: *What information do I need to do my job, from whom, when and in what form? And what information do I owe others?* He observed that these are almost never asked, and that people typically overestimate what they give and underestimate what they need.

## Communication, responsibility, trust

Drucker's account of communication inverts the usual direction. Communication, he argued, is **not what the sender says but what the receiver hears** — it is "upward", from the recipient. One can only perceive in terms of one's own experience and expectations; a message outside the receiver's range is not received at all. Communication therefore requires a shared experience and, in practice, works best when it rides on the receiver's own objectives rather than the sender's intentions.

He paired this with his treatment of **responsibility**. His preference throughout was to demand responsibility from people rather than to give them authority — authority delegated downward, responsibility demanded upward. And he distinguished trust from liking: trust is the conviction that the other person means what they say and will do what they said, which is a matter of consistency and of mutual understanding, not of affection or agreement.

## Theory of the business

In "The Theory of the Business" (*HBR*, 1994) Drucker argued that every organisation operates on a set of assumptions — about its environment (society, market, customer, technology), about its specific mission, and about the core competencies needed to accomplish that mission. Together these form the theory of the business, and they are what the organisation is actually paid for.

His specifications: the assumptions must fit reality; they must fit one another; they must be known and understood throughout the organisation; and they must be tested constantly. Organisations get into trouble, in his analysis, not because they are doing things badly but because the assumptions on which they were built have quietly stopped being true — and the response to that is almost always to do the same things harder.

## On outsourcing and support work

Drucker wrote directly about outsourcing, most prominently in "Sell the Mailroom" (*Wall Street Journal*, 1989), and his argument was about status and career rather than cost. His observation was that support work — maintenance, catering, clerical, IT operations — is permanently second-class inside an organisation whose core business is something else. The people doing it have no path, no peers who understand their craft, and no one above them who can judge the work competently.

His criterion was blunt: outsource the activities that offer no route of advancement into senior management. The example he used was college dining — as employees of a college, the people running it can never be anything but subordinates, whereas in an independent catering company they can rise to vice president or chief executive. His proposal was that such work be performed by firms for which it *is* the core business.

The reasoning runs on his own terms: the argument is not that the work is cheaper elsewhere, but that people are more productive where their work is taken seriously and where there is a career in it.

He attached a warning to the same argument. There is a price to unbundling organisations this way, and if large numbers of people cease to be employees of the organisation for which they actually work, there will be substantial social consequences. He did not claim to have resolved that tension.

## The objections to Drucker

Drucker attracts far less organised opposition than Taylor, partly because his prescriptions are harder to impose on anyone. The criticisms that exist fall into three groups.

**Method.** The most persistent charge is that Drucker was an essayist rather than a researcher. He generalised from observation, interviews and reading rather than from systematic data; his books carry few citations and almost no quantitative evidence; and critics have called him an armchair philosopher whose sweeping observations come without empirical proof. He is widely read by practitioners and comparatively little taught as research at leading business schools. Whether this is a flaw depends on what one takes management writing to be for — he described himself as a writer and a social ecologist, not a scientist — but it does mean that most of his claims are not falsifiable in the form he stated them.

**Forecasting.** Drucker made a large number of predictions over sixty years and acknowledged that several missed, saying he had lengthened the time horizon he allowed before calling a trend. His 1988 projection that the large business of twenty years hence would have half the management levels and a third the managers was directionally right about delayering and overstated in magnitude. Prediction was a substantial part of his output, and its hit rate is mixed.

**Management by objectives.** The sharpest technical attack comes from W. Edwards Deming, whose eleventh point calls for eliminating work standards and quotas on the factory floor and, explicitly, "eliminate management by objective, eliminate management by numbers and numerical goals; substitute leadership." Deming's objection was not to data — he built his career on it — but to numerical targets as instruments of management: they invite gaming, they optimise locally at the expense of the whole, they generate perverse incentives, and a target set at average performance guarantees that half the population fails it by construction.

Drucker's defenders answer that Deming is attacking the cascaded-quota version of MBO rather than the one Drucker described, which is self-set, negotiated, and paired with self-control. That defence is reasonable on the text. It is also an admission that the practice MBO became in most organisations is closer to what Deming attacked than to what Drucker wrote — which is the same gap between author and adoption that Taylor complained of, running in the other direction.

## Attributions worth checking

Two of the best-known "Drucker" lines are unreliable.

- **"Culture eats strategy for breakfast"** does not appear in his published work. It is widely attributed to him, most often traced to Ford's Mark Fields citing it in 2006. The sentiment is compatible with Drucker; the sentence is not documented as his.
- **"What gets measured gets managed"** is routinely attributed to him and is not found in his writing either; it is usually traced to V.F. Ridgway's 1956 paper on the dysfunctional consequences of performance measurement — a paper warning about measurement, not recommending it. Drucker's own position on measuring knowledge work was considerably more careful than the slogan suggests: he regarded defining the task and judging quality as the hard part, and counting output as the part most likely to mislead. From *The Effective Executive*: "Because knowledge work cannot be measured the way manual work can, one cannot tell a knowledge worker in a few simple words whether he is doing the right job and how well he is doing it."
