## Forest and Desert — Beth Andres-Beck and Kent Beck

### What it describes

Forest and Desert is a metaphor about **context and mutual unintelligibility**, not about management structure. It posits two communities of software developers living in conditions so different that advice travelling between them arrives as nonsense. The *desert* is the ordinary world of software work: bugs are plentiful, skill is not cultivated, and contact with users is difficult. The *forest* is the world of a well-run team practising something close to extreme programming: changes reach production quickly, tests make that safe, the code is invested in rather than merely extended, and the customer is a person somebody has spoken to. The problem it sets out to solve is the one that appears whenever practices are recommended across that boundary — a forest-dweller's advice is not rejected by the desert, it is heard as unrelated to anything real, and the reverse holds equally. A secondary claim carries most of its practical weight: the transition is asymmetric. Moving from desert to forest requires doing things that are both hard and counter-intuitive, while a forest degrades into desert on its own, without anyone deciding to let it.

### Why it does not act on the problems here

It is a description of the gap rather than a treatment for it, and on four of the premises this structure is built on it has nothing to offer.

**It diagnoses capability, not authority.** Forest and Desert sorts teams by the conditions they work in — test coverage, deployment speed, proximity to users. The diagnostic in [[Two profiles in a software team]] sorts by who defines the task, where the method lives, which direction measurement flows, and what happens when someone says the work should not be done. These are not the same axis. A team can be entirely forest-shaped on Beck's terms — trunk-based, well tested, deploying daily — and still receive its work pre-decomposed from above, report velocity upward, and have no path to refuse a package. The metaphor has nowhere to put that team, and that team is the one this work is about.

**Its only prescription is conversion.** The implied remedy is cultivation: grow the forest, accept that the necessary moves are counter-intuitive, and keep going. That is precisely the path ruled out here. The premise is not that the delivery group has not yet been offered broader responsibility, but that it has been, over several years, and did not take it up — and that this will not change inside the planning horizon. The move this structure makes instead is to stop trying to make the delivery group exceed its envelope and to make the envelope equal the correct outcome. Forest and Desert cannot recommend that; on its terms it reads as giving up.

**Its account of silence is the wrong one.** The metaphor explains non-communication as unintelligibility — the advice does not land because the context to receive it is absent. The premise here is different and more stubborn: the answer is thirty seconds away, the distance is one hour rather than a time zone, and the question is still not asked, because asking carries a social cost and guessing does not. Immersion, shared experience and cross-boundary pairing — the things the metaphor points toward — do not reach a barrier of that kind. The scheduled direct solicitation in section 5 exists exactly because no remedy that depends on someone speaking first is available.

**It sorts people, and this structure refuses to.** Forest and Desert names communities, and in practice names their members; the labels attach to populations within a week of entering a room. The boundary drawn here is dispositional and runs through work classes, never through locations or employers, and the failure mode it is explicitly guarded against is Class A becoming shorthand for work sent elsewhere. Imported casually, the metaphor supplies a more attractive vocabulary for the same error. Related, and disqualifying on its own for anything said on stage: a desert has no strengths. The register requirement here is that both profiles carry strengths and costs, with the first profile's decisive strength — that it does not depend on exceptional people — stated plainly. No version of this metaphor can hold that sentence.

Finally, it offers no account of origin. The desert simply exists. The degraded-Taylorism reading supplies what is missing: organisations arrive there by gradient, because input cost is trivially countable and output is genuinely hard, so measurement descends to the half that can be counted. That is a diagnosis one can act on. "You are in a desert" is not.

### What is worth keeping

Three things survive the objections.

The **asymmetry of decay** is the compressed form of the argument in section 11 for why the core team's own practice is load-bearing: a core team does not remain in the forest by intending to, and the heading *in the forest, not above it* should carry the attribution.

The **unintelligibility claim** gives a second, independent reason the solicitation cadence must ask specific people specific questions. An open "any blockers?" is a forest-shaped question; where there is no referent for it, the honest answer is no.

And the metaphor makes it possible to state plainly a position this structure otherwise only implies: **the core team has to be a forest; the delivery group does not have to become one.** Definition completeness, pre-written failing tests and executable fitness functions are the machinery that lets the two exchange work without either being converted.

### Conclusion

Forest and Desert is an accurate account of the distance and a poor instrument for crossing it. It is adopted here as vocabulary — for the decay asymmetry and for the shape of a question that cannot be heard — and rejected as a model, because its unit of analysis is the person, its remedy is conversion, and its landscape has a right side to be on.

## Lean — the Toyota Production System, and lean software development

### What it describes

Lean is the Western name — coined by John Krafcik in 1988 and carried into general use by *The Machine That Changed the World* (1990) — for the production system Taiichi Ohno built at Toyota from the 1950s onward. The problem it set out to solve was specific to a company that could not afford mass production: how to build a wide variety of products, in small volumes, to high quality, without the inventory, the buffers and the rework that a mass-production line needs in order to keep running. Its answer is flow and pull. Material moves when the next step signals for it, batch sizes shrink toward one, and buffers are deliberately removed — not as an economy, but because a buffer conceals the defect that produced the need for it, and a system without buffers is forced to surface its problems immediately. Two pillars hold this up: *just-in-time*, and *jidoka* — stopping the line the moment anything abnormal occurs, with authority to stop it given to whoever notices. Around the pillars sits the apparatus most people recognise: standardised work, kaizen, poka-yoke, the seven wastes, genchi genbutsu, the A3. The claim that matters most to this work is the one about where method lives. Lean keeps the half of [[Taylorism]] that enterprises dropped — work is studied, timed, written down and precisely specified — and then moves authorship of the standard to the people performing it. Ohno's standard is a current best known method posted at the station and revised by the line; Taylor's is produced in the planning department and issued on a card. Lean therefore occupies a cell the table in [[Two profiles in a software team]] does not have: **method explicit, precise and written down, and owned by the people doing the work.** That cell is the one [[Working structure - core and delivery group]] is building, which makes Lean the closest precedent this work has.

### Why it does not act on the problems here

**Every mechanism for discovering that something is wrong begins with somebody choosing to speak.** This is the disqualifying one and nothing else comes close. Kanban pulls material; problems are pushed. The andon cord, the kaizen circle, the suggestion system, the A3 and genchi genbutsu all start with a person deciding to raise a hand, write a card or answer a visitor honestly. Assumption 4 is that nothing is volunteered — not over a one-hour gap, not when the answer is thirty seconds away, not in a meeting convened to collect concerns. A cord nobody pulls is indistinguishable from a line with no problems, and that failure is already present and documented: remaining points carry over sprint after sprint, the product owner and scrum master record it as normal, and nobody asks what it means. Lean's answer to a cord nobody pulls is to build the conditions under which it gets pulled — respect for people, psychological safety, years of patient leadership. That is a cultural remedy on a multi-year horizon, and both halves of it are ruled out here. The scheduled direct solicitation in section 5 exists precisely because no remedy that waits for someone to speak first is available.

**It assumes repetition, and the routing rule in section 1 exists because repetition is the exception.** TPS is built where the same operation recurs thousands of times a week. That is what makes a second of motion worth removing and a written standard worth its authoring cost: the standard is written once and amortised across an enormous number of cycles. A work package is written once and executed once. Section 2 is explicit that acceptance scaffolding is unaffordable as a universal rule because writing fixtures is most of the cost of doing the work — an arithmetic that has no analogue on a line. Poppendieck's move in *Lean Software Development* was to shift the analogy from Toyota's factory to Toyota's product development system, which is the correct correction, but the vocabulary continues to carry the shop floor's evidence across a gap where it was never earned.

**Kaizen improves the work; it has no question that asks whether the work should exist.** A perfectly flowing stream of packages nobody needed is a lean success and a Drucker failure. The central move of this structure — stop trying to make a delivery group exceed its envelope and make the envelope equal the correct outcome — is a claim about what belongs inside the envelope, and nothing in the lean toolkit decides that. At Toyota the equivalent decision lives in hoshin kanri, above the team and outside the improvement cycle. Systematic abandonment belongs to [[Druckerism]]; lean optimises within given demand.

**Value-stream thinking assumes a stream you are allowed to redesign.** The standard lean diagnostic maps the flow end to end and removes the waste between the steps. Across a boundary with a separate employer, a separate management chain, contracted scope and its own commercial interest, most of the stream is not available for redesign, and proposing it reads as an attempt to manage someone else's people. The bilateral working agreement in section 6 — and specifically its *not accountable for* list — is what stands in for a stream that cannot be redrawn: it fixes the interface rather than the flow.

**Arriving as a programme, it degrades exactly as agile did.** Lean entering an organisation that measures input precisely and output not at all becomes headcount reduction, 5S audits, value-stream maps in a binder and standard work as a compliance artefact — degraded in the lean community's own vocabulary as *LAME*, lean as misguidedly executed. This is not an objection to lean. It is a second instance of the gradient in the degraded-Taylorism reading, better documented than the agile case, and it is the reason the sequence in section 13 is staged as a baseline and a first package rather than as a rollout with a name. Worth one caution for anything said on stage: the 70–90% lean-transformation failure rate that circulates in this argument has no traceable source, and practitioners who went looking for it did not find one. The pattern is real; the number is folklore.

Unlike Forest and Desert, lean fails none of the register requirements. It does not sort people, it insists that the system produces the behaviour rather than the people producing it, and its account of the first profile is more generous than this work's. Its exclusion is narrower and more specific: it answers a different question, and the one question it does share an answer to — how do you find out that something is wrong — it answers with a mechanism this arrangement has already demonstrated it will not use.

### What is worth keeping

More than from any other framing considered here.

**Poka-yoke is the precise name for section 3.** With documentation that is a maze and asking that does not happen, the apparatus is the only remaining place to put a rule. Calling ArchUnit rules, contract tests and pipeline gates *mistake-proofing* rather than *governance* changes what they are for and how they are received: the constraint is help, not audit, and a rule that cannot be broken is a rule nobody has to remember, find, or ask about. That framing is worth adopting in the document and on stage.

**Jidoka is the lineage for two rules already written.** Blocking as expected behaviour in section 5, and the core team's day-one authority to say a package is not ready in section 12, are both andon cords — one at each end of the interface. The attribution is useful because lean supplies something those rules need and do not yet have: a precedent in which stopping the line is the highest-status act on the floor rather than an admission of failure. Section 5 expects that rule to take months to hold; the lean telling of it is the best argument available while it does.

**Toyota Kata is the strongest find.** Mike Rother's coaching kata is a named coach asking a learner the same five questions on a fixed cadence — structurally the same object as the scheduled direct solicitation in section 5, arrived at independently, from a tradition managers already respect. Rother's explicit argument is that Toyota's improvement capability is a *routine* rather than a culture, and that it works with ordinary people because the questions are asked *of* them on a schedule instead of waiting for initiative. That is this work's register, stated by somebody else, in a book. Note also what it implies: the kata is the one part of lean that does not depend on volunteering, and it is the part enterprise lean programmes almost always left out.

**The queueing argument against utilisation.** Reinertsen's product-development flow supplies a non-moral case for the defended capacity split in section 4 — the first thing any manager will try to reclaim. "Slack is capacity" is a sentiment and loses the argument; a queue at ninety-five per cent utilisation has unbounded wait time and does not. The same argument gives the degraded-Taylorism section a second, independent indictment of utilisation tracking, from a tradition nobody in the room will mistake for softness.

### Conclusion

Lean is the comparison that strengthens this work rather than competing with it. It is the one system at scale that kept Taylor's knowledge half — precise, studied, written-down method — while moving authorship of the standard to the people performing it, and that is exactly what definition completeness, executable constraints and pre-written acceptance are doing here. The historical point is worth making out loud: a structure can be highly specified and not be Taylorist in the degraded sense, because what degrades is not specification but the direction measurement flows and the question of who may revise the standard.

It is adopted as vocabulary and as lineage — poka-yoke for section 3, jidoka for the two stopping rules, the coaching kata for the solicitation cadence, the queueing argument for protected capacity — and rejected as a model, for one reason that does not negotiate: every lean mechanism for surfacing a problem begins with somebody choosing to speak, and this structure exists for the case where nobody does. The rest follows from it. Lean assumes a repetition this work does not have, it optimises inside an envelope this work is trying to resize, it needs a stream this boundary does not grant, and as an enterprise programme it degrades along precisely the gradient the degraded-Taylorism reading describes — which makes its worst outcome corroboration rather than counter-example.

On stage it is one slide and a prepared answer, because somebody will ask whether this is just lean. The answer is two sentences: lean is the system that kept Taylor's knowledge half, and it is the closest thing to a precedent for what is being proposed here; it is also built on a worker who pulls the cord, and this structure is designed for the case where nobody pulls it.

## Fluid teams and demand-led planning — Daniel Terhorst-North

### What it describes

Demand-led planning is Daniel Terhorst-North's answer to the question of what an organisation does when it outgrows a single team, presented publicly as *Shaped by demand: the power of fluid teams* and described as a model developed over more than a decade of client work. Its target is the standing assumption underneath almost every scaling framework — that teams should be **stable and long-lived**, and that growth therefore means cloning a standard team shape and distributing the work across the copies. North's objection is what happens next: once the teams are permanent, the only remaining degree of freedom is the work, so demand gets cut to fit the boxes. Scarce skills and product knowledge end up in the wrong team, and the dependencies between teams become the dominant cost of the whole arrangement. His inversion is the title: *we shape the teams to fit demand*, and we do it every quarter, for a population of twenty to two hundred people. The method is eight steps. Identify **all** the demand, sorted into discretionary — delivery, discovery, kaizen — and mandatory — BAU and controls, and failure demand. Allocate the discretionary spend across those classes before any individual item is discussed. Pitch the demand to the room. Impose the constraints: teams of three to ten, **fully autonomous** — which he defines precisely as skills, knowledge, experience, *authority* and resources — no work left unstaffed, bumble bees, and optional dials for minimum and maximum movers and minimum and maximum duration. Then run around: people place themselves against the pitched demand in a big-room session, the result is tested and iterated in the room, a BHAG is declared to give the new configuration a direction, and the quarter opens with deliberate team bonding, because the teams are strangers as of that morning. The framing throughout is that organisation design builds the best structure it can, to do the best work it can, *for now* — the "for now" is what licenses the re-teaming, and the claim is that motivation and alignment rise rather than fall under it.

### Why it does not act on the problems here

**Its engine is a self-selection market, and assumption 4 says the market does not clear.** Steps 3 and 5 — pitch the demand, then run around — require people to state a preference, publicly, in a time-boxed forum, in competition with everyone else in the room. This is the open forum at its most demanding, and the premise here is that opinions appear on direct solicitation and essentially never otherwise, including in meetings convened to collect them. The failure is not that the session collapses; it is that the session **completes**. Where half the population states no preference, the unclaimed demand is allocated quietly at the side of the room, the output looks identical to a staffing spreadsheet, and it now carries a consent claim that was never given — which is strictly worse than the spreadsheet, because the next time something goes wrong the answer is that the team chose this. This is the same disqualifying shape as the andon cord in the lean entry above, and the register point from [[Two profiles in a software team]] applies unchanged: the forum is wrong, not the people. Ask those same people directly what they want to work on and a considered answer comes back.

**It presumes that autonomy is grantable, and the missing ingredient here is not grantable.** North's definition of a fully autonomous team is a formation constraint — do not form a team that lacks skills, knowledge, experience, authority or resources. It is an excellent definition and it still assumes that a properly equipped team put in front of a piece of demand will convert it into a result. Assumption 2 is that work requiring investigation produces no usable result when routed across this boundary, and assumption 3 is that this holds for the planning horizon. A team formed under North's constraint around Class C work would be fully resourced, fully authorised, and would still return nothing usable, because what is absent is not permission or capability but any wish to participate beyond the ticket. Demand-led planning is [[Druckerism]] in its purest current form — it takes the Drucker assumption as given and builds the planning apparatus on top of it. This structure exists for the case where that assumption does not hold on one side of a boundary.

**It reorganises a population this arrangement does not control.** Quarterly re-teaming across twenty to two hundred people requires a single organisation that can place all of them. Across a separate employer, a separate management chain and contracted scope, most of the population is not available to be moved by a room, and the attempt reads as an attempt to manage someone else's people. This is the same objection that rules out end-to-end value-stream redesign above, and it is why section 6 fixes the interface rather than the org chart.

**It attacks the opposite bottleneck.** North's constraint is work queued behind whichever team happens to own the code; moving people to the work dissolves it. The ceiling in section 9 is core-team authoring and classification bandwidth, and re-teaming does not move that ceiling, because the scarce thing is not where someone sits but the time of the few people who can classify and author. Quarterly re-formation makes it worse in two specific ways. Classification quality is a function of holding the landscape in your head, and the people who hold it are exactly the ones a fluid model keeps moving. And the asymmetry of decay from the Forest and Desert entry applies to the core team itself: section 12 makes the core team's internal practice load-bearing, and a group of four to six whose composition is re-pitched every quarter cannot sustain a shared practice. In fairness, the model has defences for this — the kaizen bucket keeps improvement work inside the discretionary allocation rather than leaving it to spare time, and the minimum-duration dial exists precisely to stop everything moving at once. They mitigate the tension; they do not remove it.

**Dissolving the team removes the last party who could account for a decision.** Assumption 7 is that failure produces attribution rather than investigation, because there is no shared record of what was supposed to happen. A structure in which the team that made a decision no longer exists two quarters later takes away the one party who could answer for it, and leaves attribution as the only available move. Where the counter-move is a durable record — the package, the pre-written acceptance, the fitness function — team continuity matters less; this is the one objection the structure here partially answers for itself, and it is worth noticing that it does.

Two further notes, one on register and one in the model's favour. The register risk is that demand-led planning has no place for the person whose best work is a bounded problem with a clear path and who would rather be handed one than bid for one. In a pitch-and-choose room that person does not register at all, and the implicit reading is a deficiency. The position taken here is that this is preference and not grade, and that a complete package is a better experience for that person, not a lesser one. In its favour: like lean and unlike Forest and Desert, the model does not sort people into two populations, it treats structure as the variable, and nothing in it depends on exceptional individuals except the one thing everything depends on — that somebody speaks.

### What is worth keeping

**The demand taxonomy, and failure demand as a named class.** Classification in section 1 sorts the work that arrives; North's first step insists on identifying *all* demand, including the mandatory half nobody plans — BAU, controls, and failure demand. The capacity split in section 4 is already a discretionary-spend allocation in everything but name, and it does not currently name failure demand as its own class. It should, and the volume should be a measure in section 8 rather than an interruption, because it is one of the very few output-side numbers available in this setting that is not self-referential in the way velocity is.

**Allocate the spend before pitching the items.** Step 2 preceding step 3 is the strongest procedural idea in the model for the problem section 4 exists to solve. The ratio between landscape evolution and feeding the queue is decided while it is still abstract and before any individual item is in the room to argue for itself. A split negotiated item by item is consumed by delivery every time. This belongs in the management conversation section 12 requires, and it is a better argument than any appeal to the importance of architecture.

**Autonomy defined as including authority and resources.** North's parenthetical — skills, knowledge, experience, authority, resources — is the cleanest available one-line statement of why the core team's day-one list in section 12 begins with the authority to refuse a package rather than with people or tooling. A team with skill and no authority is not an autonomous team; in this document's own phrase, it is a documentation function. Worth borrowing with attribution.

**Minimum and maximum movers as the rotation dial.** Section 11 lists as a known cost, with an ethical dimension, that nobody moves from one group to the other, and notes that even a token rotation path changes what that means at almost no cost. North's dial is exactly that mechanism, named and bounded: a stated minimum and maximum number of people who change assignment in a period, chosen deliberately rather than left to attrition. It supplies a concrete, defensible form for the one cost in the list that is otherwise only acknowledged.

**A prepared answer for the obvious question.** "Why not just move the people to the work?" is a question this talk will get, and it is a good one. Demand-led planning is the strongest, best-developed version of it, which makes it the right thing to name in the answer rather than a straw man.

### Conclusion

Fluid teams and this structure agree completely on the diagnosis and differ on one premise. Both start from the observation that standing teams force demand to be cut to fit the boxes, and that the result is work shaped around the org chart rather than the other way round. North's resolution is to re-form the teams around the demand each quarter and let people place themselves; this structure's resolution is to leave the teams where they are and make the envelope equal the correct outcome. The choice between them turns entirely on whether the population will place itself — and the whole of this work is built for the case where it will not.

It is adopted as vocabulary — the demand taxonomy with failure demand as a named class, spend allocated before items are pitched, autonomy defined to include authority, movers as a bounded dial — and rejected as a model, because every load-bearing step of it is a market that needs bids. It earns one further role that no other framing considered here does: it is the design this structure would become if assumptions 2 to 5 stopped holding. That makes it the right thing to revisit if they ever do, and the honest way to say on stage that this structure is a response to a situation rather than a belief about how software should be built.

## Dynamic Reteaming — Heidi Helfand

### What it describes

Dynamic Reteaming is a book about **team composition change**, and its argument is that the industry has the problem backwards. The standing assumption it writes against is that a team should be stable and long-lived, that composition change is a disruption to be minimised, and that every arrival or departure resets a team to the beginning of its development. Helfand's position is that change arrives regardless — growth, attrition, acquisition, boredom, a shift in strategy — so the only real choice is between change that is designed and change that merely happens, and that the second is what produces the damage usually attributed to the first. The origin is a specific experience rather than a theory: at Expertcity, after the company's first product failed in the market, Helfand was moved onto a small team deliberately separated from the rest of the organisation to build something new, and the separation is what made working differently possible. That became GoToMyPC, and the book is largely an attempt to generalise what happened. It is written out of three companies — Expertcity and its successor Citrix Online, then AppFolio, then Procore — supplemented by interviews elsewhere, and it is a pattern catalogue drawn from practice rather than a method or a study.

The catalogue is five patterns. **One by One**, where people join or leave individually and composition drifts gradually — the most common and, on Helfand's account, the most underestimated, because each single change looks too small to be worth handling. **Grow and Split**, where a team outgrows its size and divides. **Isolation**, where a team is deliberately separated from the rest of the organisation to focus. **Merging**, where two teams combine. **Switching**, where individuals move between existing teams. Around the patterns sit the supporting apparatus: ecocycle planning borrowed from Liberating Structures, applied at three levels simultaneously — the individual's experience of their team, the team as it grows and matures, and the company — and a set of participatory mechanisms for deciding who ends up where, of which the two named in the book are whiteboard reteaming and the team choice marketplace, both forms of self-selection. The stated benefits are reduced attrition risk, relief from learning and career stagnation, prevention of knowledge silos, and breaking through organisational apathy. The costs are acknowledged rather than measured: every composition change destroys unrecorded knowledge and disturbs working relationships, and the book's remedies for that are human ones — pairing, deliberate onboarding, structured integration of the new arrival.

### Why it does not act on the problems here

**Its unit of analysis is composition, and this structure's claim is that composition is not the variable.** The organising principle is that definition completeness determines the outcome: if the delivery group executes exactly the envelope it is given, a shortfall in delivery is a shortfall in the envelope. Re-sorting the same people across the same boundary leaves every envelope exactly as it was. This is not a small mismatch — the document's own "what this is not" section opens by naming reorganisation as the first of the two fatal misreadings of the proposal, the one that gets it heard as desks moving and reporting lines changing, and Dynamic Reteaming is a book about how to reorganise carefully. Adopting its vocabulary near this proposal would supply the misreading with evidence.

**Its participatory mechanisms are markets, and assumption 4 says the market does not clear.** Whiteboard reteaming and the team choice marketplace both require a person to state a preference publicly, in a time-boxed forum, alongside everyone else. This is the third framing in this document disqualified by the same sentence, after the andon cord in the lean entry and the run-around in the demand-led planning entry, and the failure mode is identical in all three: where half the population states nothing, the event still completes, the unclaimed places are filled quietly, and the result acquires a consent claim nobody gave. The register point from [[Two profiles in a software team]] holds unchanged — the forum is wrong, not the people, and the same individuals give considered answers to direct questions.

**Four of the five patterns require staffing authority this arrangement does not have.** Grow and Split, Merging, Switching and most uses of One by One presuppose a single organisation that can place the whole population. Across a separate employer, a separate management chain and contracted scope, nobody in this structure can move anybody across the boundary at any cadence, by design and by contract. Section 11 records this as a known cost with an ethical dimension. What the book cannot do is act on it.

**It treats stagnation as something done to people and movement as the relief, and here neither half holds.** "Breaking through organisational apathy" is close enough to this situation's surface description to be tempting, and the remedy underneath it assumes apathy is produced by being stuck and dissolves when the person is moved. Assumption 3 is that opportunities to grow into broader responsibility were offered over a period of years and not taken up; assumption 5 is that the posture is disengagement rather than inability, with real competence and real care about craft inside the envelope. An offer of movement to someone who does not want to move is the open invitation in another costume, and it has already been run. The register risk is the matching one: the book's implicit good is growth, change and widening scope, which reads the person who wants a bounded problem and a clean finish as stagnating. The position taken throughout this work is that this is working preference and not grade, and that a complete package is a better experience for that person rather than a lesser one.

**The knowledge-loss problem it raises is real here, and its remedies are the mechanisms already demonstrated not to run.** Helfand's answer to composition churn destroying tacit knowledge is pairing, deliberate onboarding and structured integration — all of which depend on someone choosing to teach and someone choosing to ask. Assumption 8 is that the written record is a maze harder to navigate than asking, and assumption 4 is that asking does not happen either. Every human transmission mechanism in the book sits on the wrong side of both.

On register the book itself is sound, and this should be said plainly because two of the three framings here are not. It does not sort people into populations, it is explicit that the system produces the behaviour, and it treats structure as the variable throughout. Its exclusion is narrow: it answers a question about who is on which team, in an arrangement where that question is contractually closed and would not change the outcome if it were open.

### What is worth keeping

Less than from lean or demand-led planning as vocabulary, and more than either as a diagnostic — reading this structure through the book makes two things visible that the document does not currently say.

**One by One is the pattern already running here, and the structure absorbs it by accident rather than by argument.** Delivery-group composition drifts continuously through rotation and attrition. There is no event, no announcement and no review, which is exactly Helfand's point about why the gradual pattern is underestimated: each change is too small to trigger anything. This structure happens to be the right answer to it — a new arrival receives the same complete package the departed person received, and the fitness functions enforce what that person knew — but the working structure never makes the claim. It should, because it is one of the few arguments for definition completeness that costs nothing to run and that a sceptical manager will recognise from their own staffing experience.

**Isolation is the precise name for section 12, and it comes with failure modes.** Forming a core team by separating people already working that way is Helfand's Isolation pattern, and her own account of GoToMyPC is the same move. The useful part is not the name but what the pattern is known to fail at: an isolated team losing legitimacy with the group it was drawn from, and being quietly reabsorbed once attention moves elsewhere. The day-one list in section 12 — authority to refuse a package, access to production signal, a named owner for classification — reads as countermeasures to exactly those two, which is corroboration rather than coincidence, and the warning in section 12 about being measured on authoring throughput is the reabsorption failure arriving by the route nobody blocks.

**Composition risk applies to the core team, not only to the delivery group.** Four to six people whose product is held context is the most fragile configuration in the whole design. Section 11 names key-person risk; the ecocycle's team level is the vocabulary for what a single departure does to a group that cannot function without shared understanding of the landscape. This argues for the core team's internal practice being written down in the same executable form it imposes on everyone else — the structure should not exempt itself from assumption 8.

**The regeneration gap, which is the real find.** With movement across the boundary closed, the core team has no replenishment path from the delivery group, now or ever. Section 12 says where the nucleus comes from and is silent on what replaces it in three years, which makes regeneration an onshore recruitment and development problem sitting entirely outside this structure and currently owned by nobody. That gap should be written into section 12 as a stated constraint rather than discovered later. The one mitigation consistent with the rest of the design is to let the scheduled solicitation in section 5 serve twice: a named person asking specific people specific questions every week is incidentally the only instrument in the structure that would notice somebody whose disposition has changed — somebody beginning to answer with more than was asked. An open invitation cannot produce that signal and has already failed to. This does not solve the gap; it makes "nobody ever moves" into something that would be noticed if it stopped being true, which section 11 says is wanted.

### Conclusion

Dynamic Reteaming is the least applicable framing considered in this document and the most diagnostically useful, which is an unusual combination worth being explicit about. It is inapplicable because it answers a question — who should be on which team, and how should that be changed — that this arrangement has closed by contract and that the organising principle says would not determine the outcome even if it were open. Its mechanisms for answering that question are self-selection events, which puts it on the wrong side of assumption 4 alongside every other participatory framing here. Its remedy for the cost it correctly identifies depends on people teaching and asking, which assumptions 4 and 8 rule out together.

It earns its place because of what it exposes rather than what it supplies. Three of its patterns name things this structure is already doing without having named them — Isolation for the core team's formation, the ecocycle's team level for the core team's fragility, One by One for the churn that complete packages quietly absorb. And its central concern, that composition change destroys knowledge nobody recorded, lands on the one unanswered question in the design: a core team drawn from a fixed population, with no path in from the other side, has a demographic ceiling and no stated plan. That is a finding about the working structure, produced by a book that cannot be adopted.

There is also a finding here about this document. Three framings have now been examined — lean, demand-led planning, and dynamic reteaming — from three unrelated traditions, and all three are disqualified by assumption 4 alone, before any other objection is reached. Every one of them has a mechanism for surfacing a problem or expressing a preference, and every one of them begins that mechanism with somebody choosing to speak. That convergence is worth one line on stage, because it is the clearest available argument that the scheduled direct solicitation in section 5 is not a workaround for a local difficulty but the load-bearing element of the whole design.

## Goodhart's law — Charles Goodhart, and the measurement literature around it

### What it describes

Goodhart's law is a claim about what happens to a measurement when it is used to steer the thing it measures. The original formulation is narrower and more useful than the one in circulation: *any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes.* Goodhart wrote it in 1975 about UK monetary policy, at the point where the authorities began targeting monetary aggregates and the aggregates stopped predicting what they had reliably predicted beforehand. It is a statement about a **correlation breaking**, not about people cheating. The version everybody quotes — "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure" — is Marilyn Strathern's 1997 paraphrase, written about research audit in British universities, which is why it circulates as a claim about professionals being managed by numbers rather than as a claim about statistics. The management literature got there first: Ridgway in 1956 had already set out how single, multiple and composite performance measures each produce their own distortion, nineteen years before Goodhart and in the same tradition this talk is already working in.

What it tries to solve is the problem of running an organisation on indicators. The distinction that makes it operational rather than merely quotable is that it is not one mechanism but several. Manheim and Garrabrant separate four. **Regressional** — selecting on a proxy selects on the gap between the proxy and the goal as well, which is a property of imperfect measurement and happens with nobody optimising anything. **Extremal** — the proxy and the goal travel together in the ordinary range and come apart when the proxy is pushed to an extreme. **Causal** — intervening on something correlated with the goal rather than causing it severs the correlation. **Adversarial** — an agent with different objectives optimises the measure against the party reading it. Only the fourth requires anyone to be gaming anything, and the first cannot be avoided at all. Campbell's law, often conflated with Goodhart's, is the corruption half stated separately; the Lucas critique is the same observation formalised for economic policy.

As a body of work it is a warning and not a method. There is no Goodhart-compliant design to adopt. Its entire practical output is a short list of countermeasures: do not attach numerical targets to people, which is Deming's eleventh point and already cited in [[Druckerism]]; pair measures that cannot be moved in the same direction at once; keep the measure as close to the goal as possible, ideally close enough that it stops being a proxy; and measure to understand variation rather than to control behaviour.

### Why it does not act on the problems here

It is the first framing in this document that is not disqualified by assumption 4, and the only one that is not a rival model. That makes its failure a different shape from the three above: it is not that it answers the wrong question, but that it does not answer a question at all. It disqualifies measures; it supplies no structure. On assumptions 1 to 10 it is silent, and it has nothing to say about classification, definition completeness, the routing rule or the interface. Its whole surface of contact with this work is section 8, and on section 8 it gets as far as a caution and no further.

**It is not a diagnosis of the current state, because there is nothing here to corrupt.** Goodhart's law requires a regularity that held *before* pressure was applied to it. The enterprise described in this talk has no such regularity. Velocity is not a corrupted output measure; it is the team's estimate of its own work compared against itself, a number that can rise indefinitely while nothing outside changes, and it never had a referent to lose. Sprint carry-over is a consistency measure. Utilisation and hours booked are input. The defect is the **absence** of an output measure, not the degradation of one — which is the degraded-Taylorism reading restated: all of the control, none of the knowledge. Goodhart therefore does not describe what went wrong. It describes the bill that arrives if this structure works, because section 8 is the first point in the arrangement at which a measure exists that would be worth corrupting.

**In this setting the law is more useful to the opposition than to the proposal.** "There is no point measuring that, it will just be gamed" is a principled-sounding reason never to start, and it is available to precisely the arrangement in which input is priced to the hour and output established nowhere. Section 7 is the most contestable step in the document — it is the one that asks for work before anything has been promised — and this is the cheapest objection to it. Imported carelessly, Goodhart's law hands the status quo its best argument. This is the inverse of the Forest and Desert failure recorded above: that metaphor supplied an attractive vocabulary for an error, and this law supplies an attractive vocabulary for inaction.

**Its popular register is wrong for this talk.** The widely-read version of this material argues against measurement as such. The position taken throughout this work is the opposite — that these organisations measure too little, and in the wrong direction, and that the first thing the structure buys is the ability to say what the money bought. A room that leaves with "metrics are bad" has been handed the reverse of the argument, and it will be remembered more easily than anything else said that day.

**And it names a failure without enforcing anything.** Section 8 already carries the caution, in the correct form, with the correct example. The law gets the document exactly that far. Every mechanism that would make the caution hold has to be invented here, which is the next section.

### What is worth keeping

The taxonomy, one correction, and four findings about the structure.

**The four variants, because they keep the register structural.** Regressional Goodhart needs no villain: an honest, uncoerced team degrades its own proxy simply by selecting on it. That is the same sentence as "the drift is gradient, not malice", arriving from statistics instead of from economics, and it is the thing that stops any mention of this law on stage from being heard as an accusation against whoever reports the numbers.

**Say plainly where the structure is already immune, because it is and the document never claims it.** Goodhart's severity is a function of the distance between the proxy and the goal, and the central move of this design is to close that distance to nothing at the interface. Level 4 is not a proxy for a correct outcome — red to green *is* the outcome, which is why section 2 can say minimum effort and correct outcome become identical. An ArchUnit rule does not stand in for "the boundary is respected", it constitutes it. Where the measure is the goal there is no gap for a divergence to open in, and at that point the residual risk changes species: it stops being corruption and becomes **coverage** — whatever was not encoded is now formally correct to omit — which is a cheaper failure, a visible one, and already handled by the specification-gap loop in section 6. The one real exception in section 3 is coverage and mutation gates, which are the canonical software example of this law; treating them as floors rather than as targets, which the document already does, is the correct handling and is worth saying out loud rather than leaving implicit.

**The authoring WIP cap is the document's one genuine anti-Goodhart mechanism, and it is not credited as one.** Section 12 names the structural risk — that the core team is measured on authoring throughput, because that is the only part of its output anyone upstream can see. Section 4 caps packages per period, which removes throughput as a dimension that can be optimised at all and makes the honest response to pressure — fewer, better packages, and a queue allowed to run dry — the only available one. That is a stronger class of defence than any caution, and the rest of section 8 should be held to the same standard.

**Finding: the caution in section 8 is a prose rule, in a document whose thesis is that prose rules do not hold.** Section 3 argues that any rule that matters must enforce itself, because the only signal that a document is wrong is somebody reporting confusion, and by assumption 4 that will not happen. The instruction not to turn these measures into targets is a rule carried in prose, sitting on the one page where the document is most exposed, protected by nothing but the good intentions of whoever reads it next. The structure exempts itself from its own organising principle at the precise point where it can least afford to. This is the second instance of that pattern — the first is the finding in the Dynamic Reteaming entry above, that a document built on the unusability of documents should not transmit its own core practice in one — which makes it worth stating as a general rule for the design: **wherever the structure relies on a person remembering a principle, it has reverted to the thing it replaced.**

**Finding: round trips per package and blocked time have the sign backwards.** Both are optimised by silence, and silence is this population's baseline behaviour. A block never raised contributes nothing to blocked time; a question never asked is not a round trip. Section 8 reads round trips trending up as definition quality falling, which is one reading — the other is that the delivery group has started asking. Both numbers will look their best in the quarter the interface is working at its worst, and nobody has to game anything for that to happen, which makes it regressional rather than adversarial. Underneath the two metrics is the general version: **section 8 measures the cost of asking and never the incidence of it**, in a document whose hardest-won insight is that the question does not get asked. The correction is cheap. Blocker volume belongs in section 8 as a measure in its own right, with its direction stated — in the first year a rising count is the success signal, because section 5 predicts it will take months for blocking to be believed, and that prediction is currently untestable. Package completeness defect rate has the same defect one level down: it depends on gaps being found, finding them depends on the pull cadence actually running, and it can therefore fall because the packages improved or because somebody quietly stopped making the calls. It should never be read without cadence coverage and blocker count beside it, and a defect rate falling while the blocker count falls is a bad quarter, not a good one.

**Finding: the Class A proportion and the package completeness defect rate are a paired metric, and the pairing is accidental.** Proportion-of-work-Class-A has the author, the beneficiary and the judge as the same party: the core team classifies, the core team is the party whose protected capacity in section 4 and whose capacity ceiling in section 9 are justified by the number, and the counterfactual — what class an item should have been — exists nowhere outside core-team judgement. Section 8 already flags that it is easy to improve by reclassifying rather than by improving the landscape. What it does not say is that the structure already contains the countermeasure: an inflated Class A rate shows up downstream as package completeness defects and specification gaps, both of which are also logged against the core team, so the two numbers cannot be moved in the same direction at once. Stating that pairing explicitly costs one sentence and converts a caution into a mechanism, which is the only form of protection this document otherwise accepts.

**Finding: the baseline is protected on the way in and unprotected on the way out.** Section 7 is unusually careful — recorded before it is announced, never a target, method published alongside the numbers — and every one of those protections applies to the first measurement only. The re-measurement at the end of the quarter is the evidence that decides the proposal, which means it is taken under exactly the pressure the first was shielded from, by the party whose proposal is being judged, choosing from a set of eleven numbers of which some will have moved. The fix fits what section 7 already does and costs a row: **pre-register**. At baseline time, write down which measures are the primary ones, what result would count as the structure having failed, and who runs the re-measurement — ideally not the person who proposed it. The argument for doing so is the argument section 7 already makes for the baseline itself: uncontested now, contested later.

**Finding: defect routing creates a scoreboard, and the structure does not say who keeps it.** Section 6 categorises every late defect as a specification gap or an implementation error and records it against the responsible side. Once that count has a trend line it is a two-sided score in an organisation where, by assumption 7, failure already produces attribution — and the categorisation itself becomes the contested act. The document's defence is implicit and good, and should be made explicit: the category is a property of the package text, so the determination has to be reconstructible by a third party from the artefact alone, and it has to be appealable on that basis. One asymmetry to close at the same time: specification gaps are logged against the package and never against the person who followed it, and no matching sentence protects the delivery developer when the call goes the other way.

**One line for the deck.** A developer audience knows this law and will apply it to section 8 from the floor — the question is some form of "so you have replaced velocity with eight new velocities". Getting there first is worth a sentence: that is the correct objection, it is why half of these measures are not proxies at all, it is why the two that are most exposed are paired against each other, and it is why the ones that cannot be protected are for the core team's own correction and are never reported upward as performance.

### Conclusion

Goodhart's law is not a model that competes with this structure and it is not a description of what is currently wrong with the organisation. It is a constraint on a part of the design that does not exist yet. Taken as a diagnosis it is simply inapplicable here, because the arrangement it would indict has no output measure to have corrupted — the measurement failure in a degraded Taylorist organisation is upstream of Goodhart, at the point where output was never established at all, and the law requires a regularity that once held. Taken as a slogan it is actively dangerous to this talk, because it is the most respectable available argument for never starting to measure, and it will be reached for by the people whose position section 7 threatens.

It is adopted as a **constraint on section 8 and a stress test of section 7**, and rejected as both a framing and a rhetorical device. What it produces is not vocabulary but a short list of specific repairs — blocker volume measured with its direction stated, the Class A proportion paired explicitly against package completeness defects, the baseline re-measurement pre-registered with a named owner and a stated failure condition, defect categorisation made reconstructible and appealable — and one general rule that the document had arrived at everywhere except on its own measurement page: a rule that depends on somebody remembering it has already failed. The structure's existing answer to this law is better than it knows at the interface, where pre-written tests and fitness functions are not proxies and nothing is standing in for anything, and weaker than it knows at the reporting layer, where a single paragraph of good advice is carrying the whole load. The repairs above are all cheap, and all of them are the same move the rest of the document makes: take the thing that depends on a person behaving well, and make it structural instead.

## Socio-technical systems design — the Tavistock tradition

### What it describes

Socio-technical systems design is the oldest systematic answer to [[Taylorism]] and the one that argues with it on its own ground. Its founding document is Eric Trist and Ken Bamforth's 1951 study of mechanised coal-getting in the Durham coalfield. The longwall method had been introduced on Taylorist lines — three shifts, each performing one operation, single-task roles, payment by task — and output and morale were both poor. In the Haighmoor seam the miners had organised the *same technology* differently and without being asked to: a multi-skilled group covering the whole cycle, allocating tasks internally, on a shared paybook. It performed better on every measure available. The conclusion Trist drew, and spent a decade substantiating in *Organizational Choice* (1963), is the claim the tradition rests on: **the technology does not determine the work organisation.** Given the same machinery, the same seam and the same economics, there is a real choice of structure, and in most organisations it is made unconsciously and then defended as a necessity. The problem it set out to solve is therefore not low morale and not low output but the thing underneath both — a work organisation that was adopted by default alongside a piece of equipment, and that nobody recognises as having been chosen.

The apparatus has four parts that matter here. **Joint optimisation**: a work system has a technical half and a social half, and optimising either alone degrades the whole — this is the founding claim and the reason the word carries a hyphen. **Variance control at source**: the analytic method, which identifies the *key variances* — the deviations that actually affect outcomes — and insists each be controlled in the unit where it arises rather than exported downstream to be absorbed somewhere it cannot be seen. **The semi-autonomous work group**: the design prescription, a group holding a whole and meaningful task with the discretion to self-regulate against its own variances. And a set of design principles, codified by Albert Cherns in 1976 and revised in 1987, of which four are live for this work: *minimal critical specification* — specify no more than is essential, and never the method; *boundary location* — do not draw a boundary across the flow of work, because a group that cannot see its variances cannot control them; *support congruence* — pay, measurement and reporting must reinforce the behaviour the design requires, or they will defeat it; and *incompletion* — the design is never finished. Sitting beside these is Fred Emery and Einar Thorsrud's list, from the Norwegian Industrial Democracy Programme, of six psychological requirements of a job: decision latitude, continued learning, variety, mutual support and respect, meaningfulness with line of sight to the whole, and a desirable future.

### Why it does not act on the problems here

**Its design method is a participative workshop, and it is the fourth framing in this document disqualified by assumption 4.** Emery and Emery's Participative Design Workshop — the form in which STS has been practised since the mid-1970s — is a self-managed session in which the people doing the work perform their own variance analysis and redesign their own structure over two days. The failure is the one already recorded for the andon cord, the run-around and the whiteboard, and it is identical in all four: the session completes whether or not anybody speaks, the unstated is filled in quietly at the side of the room, and the output acquires a consent claim nobody gave. That this is the oldest and best-credentialed of the four traditions, and that it fails on the same sentence before any other objection is reached, is the strongest available evidence that assumption 4 is not a local difficulty.

**Its only prescription is more autonomy, which is the conversion path assumptions 2, 3 and 5 close.** Every STS remedy runs in one direction: widen the task, multi-skill the group, move discretion down, let the unit regulate itself. Assumption 3 is that this has been offered over a period of years and not taken up, and assumption 5 is that the posture is disengagement rather than inability. STS has no design for a group that is competent, already free to self-regulate within its envelope, and does not wish to go further. Confronted with one it reads the autonomy as never having been real — which is unfalsifiable, forecloses design, and is the same move Forest and Desert makes by a different route.

**Joint optimisation requires authority over both systems, and this arrangement grants one.** The technical half — the landscape, the pipeline, the harness, the gates — is available to be changed. The social half across the boundary belongs to a separate employer with its own management chain, its own contracted scope and its own utilisation measure, and it will continue to be measured on hours booked regardless of anything agreed here. What this structure does instead is a deliberately **unilateral technical-system intervention**, chosen precisely because the "what this is not" list promises it is not contingent on anyone else changing. STS has no vocabulary for that and would name it technical determinism, the exact error the tradition was founded to oppose. The honest statement is that this is a single-sided optimisation by necessity rather than by preference, and that STS is right about what it costs.

**Its unit is the whole task, and the Class A package is deliberately a fragment.** Task fragmentation is STS's own name for the Taylorist defect, and section 1 fragments by design: outcome, classification and acceptance stay on one side, implementation crosses, production signal returns to the first side. The organising principle is what answers this — the fragment is bounded so that minimum effort and correct outcome coincide, which is what removes the harm fragmentation usually carries — but STS does not concede the point, and the tension is real rather than apparent. It is the one objection in this document that cannot be dissolved, only accepted.

**And its account of a good job is the precise form of the cost section 11 already names.** Measured against Emery's six requirements, the Class A envelope scores badly on at least four, and "nobody moves from one group to the other" fails *a desirable future* outright. This is not an argument against the structure so much as an instrument for stating its price: the list converts a cost currently carried in one sentence with an acknowledged ethical dimension into six named components, each of which can be answered or explicitly declined.

On register STS is sound and should be said to be. It is structural rather than cultural by construction — its central finding is that the same people produce different results under different organisation — it does not sort populations, and its account of why Taylorist structures persist is more generous than this work's, since it treats them as an unexamined default rather than a failure of nerve. Its exclusion is narrow and specific: it answers the question of how a group should be organised to regulate its own work, in an arrangement where one group has declined that and the other cannot be reorganised from here.

### What is worth keeping

More than from any framing considered here except lean, and of a different kind: STS supplies the rebuttal rather than the comparison.

**Minimal critical specification is the acquittal, and it is the most valuable single item in this document.** On a careless reading the principle convicts this structure of the thing the talk is about — Cherns says specify no more than is essential, and definition completeness maximises specification. The full formulation is what resolves it: identify what is essential, specify that, and specify nothing beyond it, where what is essential is the **what** and never the **how**. The package template is then worth reading against the principle field by field. Outcome, scope, out of scope, reference implementation, acceptance cases, applicable non-functionals, who to contact when blocked. There is no task breakdown, no step sequence, no instruction card and no time standard anywhere in it. The package fixes a target and a boundary and says nothing about the route. **Taylor specified the method and reserved the outcome to management; this specifies the outcome and leaves the method entirely with the person writing the code** — and section 6 removes method-adjacent obligations from the delivery group's list rather than adding them. That inversion is the answer to "this is Taylorism with better tooling", it is the single most likely hostile question the talk will receive, and it is far stronger coming from the tradition founded to oppose Taylor than from this document's own assurance. Two caveats belong with it: the reference implementation is a method hint, which is why it matters that it is offered as a resource rather than imposed; and level 4 of the ladder is where over-specification genuinely begins, which makes the existing level-4 diagnostic — if it starts feeling necessary for most packages, the problem is upstream — minimal critical specification enforcing itself, arrived at from the cost side.

**Joint optimisation is the correct name for section 3.** Moving architectural rules into the pipeline is a technical-system change made for a social-system reason: the documentation is a maze and the question does not get asked. Naming it as joint optimisation rather than as automation or governance supplies the argument for *why* rather than a description of *what*, and it is the frame least likely to be heard as control.

**Variance control at source is the organising principle in a vocabulary with seventy years behind it.** A silent assumption is exactly a key variance — a deviation arising at definition time, exported across the boundary unresolved, and absorbed downstream by a guess that surfaces as rework weeks later. Rows three to five of the case table are a variance analysis performed at the only point where it is cheap, and baseline measure 4, definition changed after work started, is a key-variance count. One difference is worth stating rather than glossed: classical STS controls a variance in the unit where it arises, and this structure instead prevents it arising at the point of execution by resolving it upstream. Eliminating a variance is a legitimate move in the tradition and the less-favoured one, and the reason it is chosen here is assumption 4 — control at source requires the unit to notice and act, and noticing without speaking produces nothing.

**Boundary location gives section 4 a principle instead of an intuition.** Cherns' rule — never draw a boundary across the flow of work — is what the paragraph on module boundaries is already claiming, and the attribution is useful because it converts an architectural preference into a design principle with evidence behind it.

**Support congruence is the missing argument in section 12.** That pay, measurement and reporting must reinforce the design or will defeat it is the general statement of why the conversation about what the core team will *not* be measured on has to happen before the team is formed, and of why section 6 warns against adding a second set of expectations on top of an employer's utilisation measure. It also sharpens the degraded-Taylorism reading considerably: input measured to the hour and output established nowhere is not merely an absent measure, it is a support system incongruent with every outcome the organisation says it wants, and it will defeat any work design placed on top of it.

**Organisational choice is the empirical evidence the talk's central structural claim currently lacks.** The sharpened thesis is that large enterprises did not choose Taylorism but grew into it. *Organizational Choice* is that claim demonstrated under controlled comparison in 1951: the same technology, two work organisations, measurably different outcomes, in an industry nobody will accuse of faddishness. It rescues the claim from sounding like an assertion about intent, and it supplies the missing third term in the history — Trist and Bamforth is 1951 and Drucker's *Practice of Management* is 1954, two post-war answers to Taylor from unrelated roots, one from psychoanalysis and open-systems biology studying coal miners and the other from management observation at General Motors, arriving at compatible conclusions. Convergence from independent traditions is better evidence than either source alone.

**A third instance of the gradient, and the best documented.** The semi-autonomous work group became "the team", the Norwegian programme was absorbed into quality of working life, quality of working life was absorbed into personnel, and Volvo's Kalmar and Uddevalla plants — the flagship demonstrations of the whole tradition — both closed in 1993. Vocabulary retained, structure reverted. This is agile's story and the lean entry's *LAME* told a third time by an older tradition, which moves the degraded-Taylorism reading from a pattern observed twice to a pattern with three independent confirmations, the earliest of them pre-dating software entirely.

**The severability finding, which is the one that changes what can be used.** There are two socio-technical traditions and they are rarely distinguished. Expert-led analytical STS, in the 1950s and 1960s, was Tavistock researchers performing variance analysis from observation and records, producing a design and handing it over. Participative design STS, from Emery and Emery in the mid-1970s onward, was an explicit reaction against that, on the grounds that expert-produced designs were sound and did not diffuse. Only the second fails assumption 4. A variance matrix can be built by an analyst from tracker history, defect records and changelogs without anybody volunteering anything — and the baseline in section 7 is structurally that exercise under another name. This makes STS the only framing examined here whose method is **severable**: the analytic half can be taken and the participative half left, and what remains is coherent rather than mutilated. It also locates this structure historically with some precision. It is closer to classical expert-led socio-technical analysis than to anything in the tradition written after 1974.

### Conclusion

Socio-technical systems design is simultaneously the closest ancestor of this structure and its sharpest critic, and the two facts are the same fact. It is the tradition that first established what this work assumes — that structure rather than people produces the behaviour, that the arrangement was never deliberately chosen, and that a technical change can be the right instrument for a social problem. It is also the tradition whose central prescription this structure declines, because every version of that prescription ends in a group regulating itself, and assumption 4 says the group will not speak and assumptions 3 and 5 say it has already declined the offer.

What separates it from the other three framings here is that its objection cannot be fully answered. Lean assumes a repetition this work does not have, demand-led planning assumes a market that does not clear, and dynamic reteaming answers a question contractually closed — all three mismatches of applicability. STS's objection is a matter of principle: the whole task belongs to one group, and this design splits it deliberately and puts a contract at the break. The defence is that the fragment is bounded so that minimum effort and correct outcome coincide, which removes the harm fragmentation ordinarily carries, and the defence is good but it is not a refutation. The honest position is that this structure accepts a cost STS names accurately, states it in section 11, and takes it because the alternative on offer has been tried.

It is adopted as vocabulary, as lineage and as defence — joint optimisation for section 3, variance control at source for the organising principle, boundary location for section 4, support congruence for section 12, Emery's six requirements as the precise form of section 11's ethical cost, organisational choice as the historical evidence for the talk's central claim, and minimal critical specification as the acquittal on the charge of Taylorism. It is rejected as a model, because its design method is a workshop that needs people to speak, its remedy is an autonomy already declined, and joint optimisation presumes authority over a social system this arrangement does not hold.

Its most useful contribution is the one the other entries cannot make. Lean is the thing somebody will ask whether this is; demand-led planning is the thing somebody will propose instead; socio-technical design is the thing somebody will accuse this of violating — and on a correct reading of its own best-known principle, it acquits. That is worth one prepared answer and two sentences: minimal critical specification says specify the *what* and never the *how*, and the work package contains an outcome, a boundary and a set of acceptance cases, and no instruction anywhere about how to write the code.

- *The Principles of Sociotechnical Design* — Albert Cherns, *Human Relations* 29(8), 1976, revised as *Principles of Sociotechnical Design Revisited*, *Human Relations* 40(3), 1987. The nine principles, including minimal critical specification, variance control, boundary location, support congruence and incompletion. The 1987 revision is the better text.
- *Socio-Technical Design* — Philip Herbst (Tavistock, 1974). Minimal critical specification is often attributed here as well as to Cherns; Cherns is the codification, Herbst the fuller treatment.
- *Form and Content in Industrial Democracy* — Fred Emery and Einar Thorsrud (Tavistock, 1969), and *Democracy at Work* (Martinus Nijhoff, 1976). The Norwegian Industrial Democracy Programme, and the source of the six psychological requirements of a job.
- *Participative Design: Work and Community Life* — Fred Emery and Merrelyn Emery (Centre for Continuing Education, ANU, 1974). The participative turn, and the explicit argument against expert-led redesign on the grounds that good designs produced by consultants did not diffuse. This is the document that creates the split described under *the severability finding* above.
- *Sociotechnical Systems: A North American Reflection on Empirical Studies of the Seventies* — William Pasmore, Carole Francis, Jeffrey Haldeman and Abraham Shani, *Human Relations* 35(12), 1982. The standard review of the empirical record; useful because it is a count rather than an advocacy piece.
- *A Reappraisal of Sociotechnical Systems Theory* — John Kelly, *Human Relations* 31(12), 1978. The critical counterweight, and the one to read for symmetry: Kelly argues that the productivity gains attributed to autonomy are better explained by changes to payment systems and by work intensification. It stands in the same relation to STS as Braverman does to [[Taylorism]] and as Deming's eleventh point does to [[Druckerism]].
- *Designing Human Systems* — Enid Mumford (1983), and *The Story of Socio-Technical Design: Reflections on its Successes, Failures and Potential*, *Information Systems Journal* 16(4), 2006. Mumford carried STS into information systems design as the ETHICS method; the 2006 paper is her own post-mortem on why the tradition declined, and is the most useful single source for the degradation argument above.
- *Sociotechnical Principles for System Design* — Chris Clegg, *Applied Ergonomics* 31(5), 2000. The modern restatement, nineteen principles, and the usual bridge cited when the term appears in software contexts.
- *The Social Engagement of Social Science: A Tavistock Anthology* — Eric Trist and Hugh Murray (eds), three volumes (University of Pennsylvania Press, 1990–97). Volume 2 collects the socio-technical papers, including the 1951 study and Trist's 1981 retrospective *The Evolution of Socio-Technical Systems*. Made freely available online by the Tavistock Institute.
- *Enriching Production* — Åke Sandberg (ed., Avebury, 1995), on Volvo Uddevalla and the reflective-production alternative. Christian Berggren's *Alternatives to Lean Production* (1992), already cited in the lean entry above, covers Kalmar and Uddevalla from the same angle and stands in both places.

Evidentiary notes. The 1951 study was small-n action research conducted by researchers who were also advocates and, later, consultants to the industry they were studying — the same evidentiary caution applied to the pig iron story in [[Taylorism]] and to Hawthorne belongs here, and *Organizational Choice* (1963) is the controlled comparison that answers it. Kelly's 1978 reappraisal is listed above for the same reason and should be acknowledged rather than omitted if the tradition reaches the deck. The distinction between expert-led and participative socio-technical design is drawn in this document from the chronology and from Emery and Emery's own stated motivation; it is a reading, not a claim either tradition makes about itself, and should be presented as such. Finally, a caution about the word: *sociotechnical* in current software usage — DevOps writing, *Team Topologies* and adjacent literature — almost always means no more than "the people matter too" and carries none of the analytic method described here. If the term reaches the deck it needs the 1951 referent attached, or it will be heard as the empty version.
